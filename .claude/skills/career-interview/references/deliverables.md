# Turning the profile into documents

Read this before writing any CV or LinkedIn content. The master profile is the source; every document is a selection from it, never a fresh invention.

## The order of work

0. **Agree the voice first.** Before writing a full document, draft *only the summary* and run a tone check with them (below). Register is the one thing you cannot infer from the profile and the one thing they will notice immediately. Getting it wrong means rewriting every section; getting it right first means the rest of the document writes itself in their voice.
1. **Master CV** — generous, 2–4 pages, everything relevant. Never sent anywhere; it's the cutting board.
2. **Targeted CV** — cut from the master for one posting or one class of role. This is what gets sent. One page unless they chose two (see [Length](#length-one-page-by-default)).
3. **LinkedIn** — a different genre: public, first-person, searchable, read by humans browsing rather than screening.
4. **Cover letter / outreach** — only when it's asked for or when they particularly want the role.
5. **Interview stories** — the Challenge/Action/Result entries from the profile, expanded, so the document and the conversation agree.

Doing it in this order means each document is a filtering decision rather than a rewrite, and the CV and LinkedIn never contradict each other — which recruiters do check.

## The tone check

Run this before writing the body of the first document, and again on any document whose register they query.

**Draft the summary in two contrasting registers** — say, plain first-person reporting ("I've spent six years at…") versus third-person positioning ("Product analyst who builds…") — show both, and ask which sounds like them. Two is enough; three invites paralysis. Then ask the real question: *"rewrite the first line the way you'd actually say it."*

**Their rewrite is the specification.** When someone hands you a reworded line, copy the *construction*, not just the words — person, tense, sentence length, whether they hedge, whether they use rhetorical setups. One rewritten sentence tells you more about their register than any amount of inference from the interview transcript. Record it in the profile as a voice sample and write everything else to match.

**Then read the summary back and ask directly whether it sounds like them.** People will accept an accurate document that sounds wrong to them and then quietly never send it. Asking costs one exchange.

### Register failures to avoid

These are the constructions that make a document read as machine-written. They are worth knowing by name because people notice the effect ("it sounds robotic", "this reads like a LinkedIn post") long before they can name the cause, and the fix is structural rather than lexical.

- **The post structure**: a rhetorical setup followed by a punchy payoff. *"Most of my work answered one question. The customers were there, the product was there, but onboarding lost half of them. So I rebuilt the first week."* Fine in a blog post, wrong in a CV, where the reader wants reporting rather than an argument with a turn in it. Replace with plain declarative sentences.
- **The tricolon**: three parallel items, especially three gerunds. *"Comfortable working with clinicians, managing vendors, and delivering under pressure."* One list of three concrete things (products, systems, domains) is fine; a list of three *qualities* is the single most recognisable tell. Break it into two clauses or two sentences.
- **Adjective openers**: "Comfortable with…", "Passionate about…", "Experienced in…". Claims about temperament rather than record. Use a verb in the first person instead.
- **Fragment metadata endings**: closing with stubs like *"Bilingual Spanish/English. Promoted twice."* Two fragments after a long sentence read as a machine appending fields. Join them into one clause.
- **Circumlocution**: *"I'm used to scoping directly with the people who'll use the thing"* is eleven words doing five words' work, and the hedge at the front drains the claim. Find the precise word, such as "users" for "the people who will use it" or "upstream" for "the teams the data comes from", and cut the scaffolding. Density reads as confidence.
- **Pseudo-precise abstraction**: a coined phrase that sounds like a term of art but names nothing the person actually did. *"I bring structure to ambiguity"* — they wrote the first incident runbook for a team that had none; "structure to ambiguity" was the writer's invention. This is the most dangerous failure in the list, because density is a virtue and these phrases mimic it. Test every compressed phrase against the profile: if you cannot point to the specific thing it refers to, cut it. A summary generally should not carry an abstract claim about *how* someone works when a bullet already carries the concrete instance.
- **Labelled bullets**: an invented headline followed by a colon and then the facts. *"Turned around a failing launch: with the vendor two months late, re-planned the rollout…"* The label is the writer editorialising about the candidate, and it delays the content by a clause. State what happened, verb first: *"Re-planned the rollout around the late vendor, so the first two regions launched on the original date."* Bold the opening phrase if it needs emphasis, but make that phrase a fact rather than a title.
- **Third-person employer context lines**: *"Acme. Builds and runs the payments platform behind Acme Pay."* Grammatically this describes the employer, but on a page written in the candidate's voice it reads as someone else narrating them, and the register switch is jarring. Use a participle instead: *"Acme. Building and running the payments platform behind Acme Pay, serving merchants in 12 countries."* One voice from the summary to the last bullet.
- **Em dashes**: see the bullet rules below.

The correction is precision, not ornament. Inflated diction reads worse than flat diction, because it looks like reaching. Aim for someone confident and slightly terse.

## Writing a bullet

The structure that carries the most information per line:

> **[Strong verb] + [what, with scope] + [result, with number] + [constraint or method if it adds difficulty]**

- *Weak:* Responsible for the reporting pipeline.
- *Better:* Rebuilt the reporting pipeline, cutting refresh time from six hours to twenty minutes.
- *Strong:* Rebuilt a reporting pipeline serving 200+ analysts, cutting refresh from 6h to 20min and retiring $90K/yr of warehouse spend — with no reporting downtime during the cutover.

Working rules:

- **Lead with the outcome when it's impressive, the action when the outcome is soft.** Front-loading matters because bullets are scanned, not read.
- **One accomplishment per bullet.** Two unrelated wins merged into one bullet read as neither. Parts of the same story are different; see [Fitting to one page](#fitting-to-one-page).
- **Bold sparingly, and mostly on the numbers.** Bold works by contrast, so a page where a third of the words are bold emphasises nothing and buries the figures. Structural bold (name, section headings, employer, title) is standard and unrestricted. Inside bullets, reserve it for the quantified result, plus at most one anchor phrase on the lead bullet of a role. A useful target is under about 15% of body words; a useful test is to read only the bold and check it still delivers the numbers, which is roughly what a thirty-second skim does.
- **Numbers early.** A number in the first half of the line survives skimming; one at the end often doesn't.
- **Scope counts as a number.** "Across 40,000 monthly signups" does real work even with no lift figure.
- **Keep difficulty in** when it's genuine — the constraint is usually what makes the accomplishment non-generic.
- **Match the claim to the evidence tag.** `[doc]` and `[stated]` items can be asserted plainly. `[est]` items should be rounded and softened ("roughly", "~"). `[unverified]` items do not go in until confirmed — flag them to the user instead of quietly including them.
- **Their voice, tightened.** Pull phrasing from the `[verbatim]` captures. A CV written entirely in resume-ese sounds like everyone else's.
- **3–6 bullets for recent and relevant roles, 1–2 for old or off-target ones.** The taper is itself a signal about what they do now.
- Avoid: "responsible for", "helped with", "various", "utilised", adjectives without evidence ("dynamic", "results-driven"), and stacked buzzwords.
- **No em dashes (—) in anything the candidate sends out.** Readers increasingly treat them as a signature of AI-generated text, and a recruiter who suspects the CV was generated discounts everything on it. This costs you nothing: an em dash is always replaceable. Use a colon when what follows explains or delivers a result ("cut month-end close from ten days to four: adopted by every regional finance team"), a comma for an appositive or a trailing clause, a semicolon to join two independent statements, parentheses for a genuine aside, or a full stop where the sentence was doing too much. Applies to the CV, LinkedIn, cover letters and outreach. En dashes in date ranges (2021–2024) are ordinary typography and are fine.

## Awkward history: structure, not explanation

Handle retitles, reorgs, gaps, short stints and sideways moves through the layout: dates, grouping roles under one employer, and what each entry describes. Don't write sentences explaining them. An explanation sounds defensive and points the reader straight at the thing you wanted them to skim past.

- **Describe the work, not the title.** A retitled role gets a description of what they did in it, like every other entry. Never write "same work, new title" or "renamed to reflect…".
- **Never name what they didn't do.** "…rather than order processing" puts order processing in the reader's head, right next to their name.
- **Interview answers stay out of documents.** The profile keeps "if asked, say…" lines about awkward bits, tagged `[interview only]`. They're for a conversation where someone has already asked. Never paste them into a CV or LinkedIn.
- **The only acceptable note is a bare fact the timeline needs**, such as "Career break, 2019–2020". Add no reason unless they chose to give one.

*Wrong:*
> Same work, more accurate title — the whole team was regraded to reflect that we were doing account management rather than order processing.

*Right:* drop the sentence and start with the work.
> Managed a book of 60 wholesale accounts, including peak-season stock allocation for the five largest.

## CV structure

Recruiters in the Forbes panel consistently rewarded clean sectioning and consistently punished density — the most common criticism was simply too much text on the page. Two columns, icons, colour blocks and photos were called dated and also confuse parsers. So:

```
Name
City · phone · email · LinkedIn · [portfolio/GitHub]

SUMMARY            3–4 lines: who they are, scope, the two or three things
                   they're known for, aimed at the target role. No objective
                   statement, no "seeking a challenging position".

[CORE SKILLS]      Optional single band of 8–14 real, searchable terms.
                   Only skills they'd survive a question on.

EXPERIENCE         Employer · Title · Dates · Location
                   One line of context: what the company does, its size,
                   and their mandate — this is where scope lives.
                   • bullets, most impressive first

EDUCATION          Degree, institution, year. Honours if notable.

[ADDITIONAL]       Certifications, patents, publications, talks, languages,
                   volunteering — whichever are relevant.
```

Reverse chronological unless there's a strong reason otherwise — functional resumes read as concealment to most recruiters.

### Length: one page by default

Before drafting the CV that gets sent, ask once whether they want one page or two, and recommend one. It's a logistics choice, so a quick multiple-choice question is fine. One page forces the selection that makes a CV readable. Two is reasonable for well over a decade of senior, relevant experience. Academic and medical CVs follow their own conventions. Record the answer in the profile's constraints and decisions section so later drafts don't ask again.

### Fitting to one page

Merge first, then cut. Shrinking the font and margins comes last, and only a little: the recruiter panel's most common complaint was too much text on the page.

**Merge** bullets that are parts of one story. The shared setup gets written once, and two half-empty last lines become one full one.

- **Same problem, several fixes.** Two automations of the same manual reporting chore become one bullet: "Automated two reports the team had compiled by hand — the weekly one now runs in ten minutes instead of a day, and the monthly one has not had an error since."
- **Scope into the accomplishment it measures.** A "wrote the training materials" bullet folds into the system rollout bullet it belongs to.
- **Follow-on work into the main work.** Self-service onboarding built on top of a tool goes in that tool's bullet.
- **Consecutive titles at one employer** doing similar work share one heading ("Associate, then Manager · 2019–2022") and one set of bullets. The "then" keeps a promotion visible.
- **Small sections into one line.** Languages, publications, awards and interests go on a single Additional line, separated by dots. A secondary school folds into the degree line or goes.
- **Say each thing once.** A fact in the summary doesn't get repeated in an employer's context line.

A merge works if the bullet's opening words can name what the parts share. If you can't name it, they're two bullets. A merged bullet should fit in two lines, three at most. If it runs longer, cut instead.

**Then cut**, least relevant first: the weakest bullet in the least relevant role, older roles down to one line (or a single "Earlier roles" line), the summary down to two or three lines.

**Check the rendered page, not the markdown.** If you can produce the PDF, do, and look at it. If you can't, tell them to check the page count when they export.

Send PDF unless the posting explicitly asks for .docx. Name the file `Firstname-Lastname-CV.pdf`, not `resume-final-v3.pdf`.

## What ATS actually does

Get this right, because over-optimising for imagined robots produces worse documents for the humans who actually decide.

**What's true:**
- The ATS parses the document into structured fields — contact, employers, titles, dates, skills — and stores it in a searchable database.
- Recruiters then search and filter that database, overwhelmingly by keyword: titles, skills, tools, credentials. This is the real reason keywords matter. You are trying to appear in a recruiter's filtered list, not defeat a bot.
- Bad parsing is a genuine failure mode: text in headers/footers, images or text boxes, multi-column layouts, tables, and unusual date formats all get mangled or dropped.
- An increasing number of pipelines add an AI summarisation and ranking layer on top of the parse, which rewards a document whose relevance is legible in plain language.

**What's overstated:**
- ATS software rarely auto-rejects on content. Humans do the rejecting; the software sorts and surfaces. Missing one keyword does not delete an application.
- There's no keyword density target, and keyword stuffing is visible to the human who reads next.
- "ATS-friendly template" is largely a marketing term. Plain, single-column, standard headings, real text — that's the whole requirement.

**Practical implications:** single column; standard section headings ("Experience", not "Where I've Made an Impact"); contact details in the body, not the header; no text in images; spell out an acronym once alongside its expansion ("Search Engine Optimisation (SEO)") so either search term matches; use the exact job titles and tool names the target postings use, where they're honestly applicable.

## LinkedIn, section by section

LinkedIn is not the CV pasted into a website. It is public, first-person, permanently indexed, and read by people deciding whether to reach out rather than whether to reject. It's also read in a skim, often on a phone, with most text hidden behind "see more". Write short.

**Headline** — the most valuable 220 characters on the profile: it travels with every search result, comment and message, and it's weighted heavily in search. Use the target job title in the words recruiters actually search, plus specialism and a differentiator. `Senior Data Engineer | Streaming & Real-Time Pipelines | Built the platform behind 4M daily events`. Avoid a bare current title, and avoid "Passionate about…". If remote or a specific location is a hard requirement, say so here — recruiters filter on it.

**About** — first-person, 3–4 short paragraphs, roughly 150–200 words. The 2,600-character limit is a ceiling, not a target. The first two lines are all that shows before "see more", so put the positioning statement there. Then what they do and the scope they do it at, two or three proof points with one number each, what they're looking for, and how to reach them. The full story of each project belongs in Experience, or in the interview. Write it the way they'd actually introduce themselves; the profiles that read as human outperform the ones that read as a resume summary.

**Experience** — the same accomplishments as the CV, in first person, and no longer. Having no page limit means roles the CV cut for space can stay. It doesn't mean more words per role. Only the first two or three lines show before "see more", so open with one line on what the role is, plus what the company does if readers might not know it. Then:

- Current and recent roles: 3–5 bullets, one or two lines each, one number per bullet.
- Older or off-target roles: one to three lines, no bullets.
- Leave out implementation detail (service names, architecture like "a queue-backed worker pool over…", evaluator chains) unless the term is one a recruiter would search for. Save that detail for interviews.

Job titles should match the CV exactly — mismatches get noticed.

**Cut before you show it.** Delete every clause that explains how something worked rather than what changed, every second number in a bullet, and every sentence a recruiter skimming on a phone wouldn't miss. A first draft usually loses a third.

**Skills** — 10–20 genuinely relevant ones, not fifty. Pin the three most important to the target. This section is explicitly searched and filtered, so it should contain the exact terms from target postings. Endorsements and any verified-skill badges add weight, so it's worth asking a few colleagues.

**Featured** — the most under-used section. A portfolio piece, a talk, a published article, a project, a press mention. Anything here is evidence rather than assertion.

**Recommendations** — worth actively requesting from two or three people, ideally naming for each what you'd like them to speak to. A recommendation describing a specific outcome outperforms a warm general one.

**Open to work** — the recruiter-only setting is visible to LinkedIn Recruiter subscribers without showing the green frame publicly. If their current employer shouldn't know, use that setting, and turn off activity broadcasts before rewriting the profile so the edits aren't announced.

**Also:** a real photo and a banner; a custom URL; the location field set to the market they want to work in; the About and Experience sections carrying the same vocabulary as the target postings.

### Writing to the live profile

When you can edit the profile directly (browser tools), you are publishing under their name.

1. Draft everything in `career/linkedin-rewrite.md` first. Rationale and notes stay in that file. Only the text meant for the profile goes in the blocks you'll paste.
2. Turn off *Share profile updates with your network* before the first edit, so the rewrite isn't broadcast.
3. Before saving any field, show them the exact text going into it and get an explicit yes for that text. You can batch several fields into one approval. Agreeing to the plan, or to the draft file as a whole, is not approval of the words.
4. Never delete an entry or change a title or date unless they told you to.
5. After saving, re-read the live field, then log what changed in `interview-state.md`.

## Tailoring to a specific posting

1. Read the posting and extract its vocabulary: required skills, tools, the exact job title, recurring nouns.
2. Check those against the profile's skills inventory and accomplishments. Where there's genuine evidence, make sure the document uses the posting's word for it, not a synonym.
3. Reorder and reselect bullets so the top three in the most recent role speak to what this posting leads with.
4. Rewrite the summary for this role specifically — it's four lines, it should be bespoke every time.
5. Prune anything irrelevant. Cutting is what makes tailoring visible.
6. Never add a skill or claim that isn't in the profile. If the posting wants something they don't have, that's a coverage decision for them to make, not a wording problem to solve.

## Before you hand anything over

- Every number traceable to the profile, with `[est]` ones softened and `[unverified]` ones absent.
- Dates and titles consistent between CV, LinkedIn and anything else.
- Nothing included that Phase 6 said to withhold.
- No sentence that explains or justifies a title, gap or move, and no `[interview only]` material.
- LinkedIn About and Experience within the lengths above.
- The CV fits the agreed page count as rendered, with no merged bullet over three lines.
- Read the top third aloud — if it could describe fifty other people, it's not done.
- Tell them plainly which claims they should be ready to defend in an interview, and which artifacts would make those claims solid.
