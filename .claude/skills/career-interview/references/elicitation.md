## The core technique

Forensic psychology has spent forty years on the problem of getting accurate, detailed recall out of a cooperative person who genuinely cannot remember much on demand. The cognitive interview is the result, and it beats standard questioning substantially on quantity of accurate detail. Four of its components transfer directly to career interviewing:

**Context reinstatement.** Before asking what someone achieved in a role, put them back in it. "Take me back to your first month at Acme — where were you sitting, who was on the team, what was the thing everyone was worried about?" Sensory and social context are retrieval cues for everything attached to that period. This single move is the difference between "I did backend work" and a specific memory of the outage that started the project.

**Free recall before probing.** Open each role with an invitation to talk uninterrupted — "walk me through those two years, as much detail as you like, in any order" — and do not interrupt for the first minute or two. Their unprompted narrative tells you what mattered to them, which is where the good material is. Probe *after*, using their own words as the hooks.

**Report everything.** Say explicitly that details they think are irrelevant are welcome, because you will do the filtering. People suppress exactly the specifics that make a bullet concrete — the number of stores, the name of the system, the fact that it was during a hiring freeze — because they have decided those don't belong on a resume. Take that decision away from them.

**Change of order or perspective.** When a role has gone flat, try "what would your manager have said was the hardest thing you fixed that year?" or walk it backwards from their last week to their first. Different retrieval routes surface different material.

The one thing to steal from the interrogation literature only in reverse: leading questions contaminate recall. See [Don't contaminate the record](#dont-contaminate-the-record).

## Getting past duty-talk

The universal first answer is the job description. "I was responsible for the reporting pipeline." That sentence contains no information a recruiter can use — a hundred other candidates were also responsible for a reporting pipeline.

Your move is always some version of **"and what changed because of that?"** Vary the phrasing so it doesn't feel like a tic:

- "What was different afterwards?"
- "What did that make possible that wasn't possible before?"
- "If you hadn't done it, what would have happened?"
- "Who noticed?"
- "What was the state of it when you took it over, and when you handed it on?"

The before/after framing is the most reliable of these. People who cannot answer "what did you achieve" can almost always answer "what was it like when you arrived, and what was it like when you left."

Two more that break duty-talk open:

- **"What were you actually hired to fix?"** — Almost every role exists because something was wrong or something needed building. That is the challenge half of every accomplishment, and it's usually one sentence away.
- **"What was hard about it?"** — Difficulty is where the differentiation lives. Anyone can run a migration; running one with no downtime window, during a hiring freeze, on a system nobody had documented, is a story.

## The metric ladder

When you have a result but no number, climb until you find one. Stop as soon as something lands; do not run the whole list.

1. **Direct measure.** "Do you know roughly what that moved?" — revenue, cost, time, error rate, conversion, headcount, churn, NPS, uptime, throughput.
2. **Scope as substitute.** If the outcome can't be measured, size the thing it happened to: how many users, how many stores, how big was the codebase, how many countries, what was the budget, how many people were on the team. Scope is a legitimate and under-used number. "Rebuilt onboarding for 40,000 monthly signups" says plenty even with no lift figure.
3. **Before and after.** "What was it before? What was it after?" People who can't produce a percentage can often produce two absolute numbers, and you can compute the delta in front of them.
4. **Time and frequency.** "How long did it take before? How long after? How many times a week did that happen?" Multiply: four hours saved, weekly, across six analysts, is a number.
5. **Comparison.** "How did that compare to the previous year / the other regions / what was forecast / what your predecessor managed?"
6. **Counterfactual.** "What would it have cost to keep doing it the old way?" or "what was the risk you avoided?"
7. **Social proof.** No number at all? Take the recognition instead: a promotion, an award, being asked to run it again at a bigger scale, a customer quote, a line from a performance review. "Cited in the year-end review as the reason the region hit target" is evidence.

Always ask **"how do you know?"** after a number lands. The answer tells you whether it's dashboard-solid, remembered, or guessed — which determines how it gets tagged in the profile and whether they can defend it in an interview.

## When they say there are no numbers

This is the most common blocker and it is usually wrong. Work through, in order:

- **Normalise it.** "Most people say that, and we usually find three or four. Let's try a couple of angles." Removing the sense that they are failing a test matters more than the technique that follows.
- **Ask what they had access to.** "What dashboard, report or review did you look at for this?" They may not remember the figure but they remember the instrument, and often they can still go and get it.
- **Ask what their boss bragged about.** Managers report upwards in numbers. "When your director talked about your team at the quarterly review, what did they cite?"
- **Size the environment instead.** If the contribution can't be sized, size the context (ladder step 2).
- **Accept a bounded estimate.** "Would you say it was closer to 10% or closer to 50%?" Anchoring on a range gets an answer where an open question gets a shrug. Record it explicitly as an estimate, and tell them you're doing that: "I'll note that as approximate so you're never defending a number you didn't verify."
- **Leave it and move on.** A vivid, specific, unquantified accomplishment beats a stalled interview. Park it in the gaps list with a note on what would confirm it.

Never resolve the blockage by supplying a number yourself, not even as a suggestion they might agree to. A figure the interviewer invented and the candidate nodded at is the worst kind of resume claim.

## Questions that reliably unlock material

Keep these in your pocket for any role, any level. They out-perform structured questioning surprisingly often.

- "What are you proudest of from that time — not necessarily the biggest thing, the one you'd actually tell a friend about?"
- "What did you do there that nobody else could have done?"
- "What did people come to you for, regardless of what your job title said?"
- "What broke after you left?"
- "What's the thing you fixed that nobody ever noticed because you fixed it?"
- "What was the meeting where you changed someone's mind?"
- "If I called your best colleague from that job, what story would they tell about you?"
- "What did you get promoted for?" — promotion criteria are pre-quantified accomplishments.
- "What's on your resume from that job that undersells what actually happened?"

## Don't contaminate the record

You are the author of the document but not the source of the facts, and it is easy to put words in someone's mouth without noticing.

- **Don't offer the answer inside the question.** "So you'd say you improved efficiency significantly?" produces agreement, not information. Ask "what happened to efficiency?"
- **Don't propose a number to be confirmed.** Bounded estimates ("closer to 10 or closer to 50?") are fine because they're symmetric and explicitly approximate. "Was it about 30%?" is not.
- **Don't upgrade their language silently.** If they say they "helped with" something, do not record "led". Ask: "what was your part in it, specifically — did you own it, run part of it, or contribute to it?" The answer is usually more impressive than "helped with" and honest in a way "led" wouldn't be.
- **Don't merge two events into one better story.** Tempting, and a serious problem when they're asked about it live.
- **Do mirror back verbatim.** "You said it was 'the thing everyone was scared to touch' — can I use that phrasing?" Their words, confirmed, are safe.
- **Confirm arithmetic you did yourself.** If you turn "we went from about nine to about eleven" into "roughly a 20% improvement", that percentage is *your* claim until they agree to it. Say the derived figure back and get a yes before it enters the profile. This is the easiest way for an invented number to slip into an otherwise honest document, because it feels like summarising rather than inventing.

## Pacing, rapport and knowing when to stop

- **Acknowledge before probing.** One short reaction to what they just said, then the next question. Pure question-after-question reads as an interrogation and answers get shorter.
- **Signal position.** "That's the last thing on Acme, then we move to Globex" keeps people going. Not knowing how long something will last is what makes it tiring.
- **Three probes is usually the limit** on a single accomplishment. If the third one doesn't land, park it and move on; you can return later, and often they'll come back to it unprompted.
- **Watch for the shift.** Longer, faster, more specific answers mean you've hit something real — abandon the plan and stay. Shorter, flatter, "I guess so" answers mean fatigue or a dead end — change topic or offer a break.
- **Offer breaks at phase boundaries** on anything longer than 45 minutes, and save state when you do.
- **Don't fill silence.** After a hard question, wait. In text, that means asking the question and stopping — no helpful list of examples appended, which just narrows what they'll say.

## Sensitive history

Gaps, firings, layoffs, short stints, illness, caregiving, visa constraints, a pivot that looks like a demotion. These need to be on the record because they shape the document, and people tend to be braced for judgment.

- **Raise them flatly, as logistics.** "I see about eight months between Acme and Globex — what was going on there? I'm asking because it's better to have a deliberate way of presenting it than to leave it unexplained."
- **Name the standard handling immediately** so they know it's a solved problem: date granularity, grouping contract roles under one heading, a one-line factual note (the fact, never the reason), or simply not addressing it in the document at all. Any explanation they'd give if asked goes in the profile as `[interview only]`, not in the document.
- **Don't ask for reasons you don't need.** For a medical gap you need the duration and whether they want it mentioned. You do not need the diagnosis.
- **Ask what they want disclosed**, separately for CV and LinkedIn — LinkedIn is public and their current employer may be reading it.
- **Take contract-versus-permanent labelling seriously.** Marking roles as contract can cost candidates against full-time peers; marking them wrong is a misrepresentation. Ask how they want it shown and note the trade-off once.
- **Their call, always.** Record the decision in the profile so later drafts don't relitigate it.

## Calibrating by seniority and personality

- **Early career:** the material is in internships, coursework projects, part-time work, volunteering and side projects, and they will dismiss all of it. Ask about scale and responsibility in those settings rather than titles.
- **Deep individual contributors:** they under-claim collaboratively-produced work. Separate "what the team shipped" from "what your hands did" explicitly, and ask about technical judgment calls — the decisions they argued for are their differentiator.
- **Managers:** push past team achievements to what they specifically changed — hiring, restructuring, process, retention, people they grew. "What did the team look like when you took it over?" is the workhorse question.
- **Executives:** scope and transformation. P&L, headcount, board and investor exposure, market position before and after, what the company could do afterwards that it couldn't before.
- **Career changers:** dig for the transferable substrate — the underlying skills, the domain knowledge, the evidence of learning speed — and get the honest version of why they're moving, because the narrative depends on it.
- **The modest:** attribute to others to unlock. "What would your manager say?" gets an answer where "what did you achieve?" gets deflection.
- **The expansive:** let the first free-recall pass run, then narrow hard to specifics, dates and numbers. Their risk is unverifiable colour, not silence.
